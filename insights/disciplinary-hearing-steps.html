What Happens at a Disciplinary Hearing? A Step-by-Step Guide

From the investigation to the final sanction, here is how a fair disciplinary process works and what both sides should expect.

Discipline & Disputes · 27 November 2026 · 3 min read · By Labour Law Alliance

A disciplinary hearing is how an employer fairly decides whether an employee is guilty of misconduct and, if so, what sanction is appropriate. The Labour Relations Act and its Code of Good Practice: Dismissal require both a valid reason and a fair procedure. The hearing does not have to be as formal as a court, but it must give the employee a genuine opportunity to respond.

1. Investigation

Before any charges are laid, the employer should gather the facts: statements from witnesses, documents, footage or system records. An investigation helps decide whether there is a case to answer and what the charges should be.

2. Precautionary suspension (if needed)

Where there is a real risk that the employee could interfere with the investigation or the workplace, the employer may suspend them on full pay while the matter is investigated. A precautionary suspension is not a sanction and should not drag on unreasonably.

3. Notice of the hearing

The employee should receive written notice that sets out:

  • the charges, in enough detail to understand the allegations;
  • the date, time and place of the hearing;
  • the right to be represented by a fellow employee or a shop steward;
  • the right to call witnesses, present evidence and use an interpreter.

The employee must have a reasonable time to prepare. Many disciplinary codes provide for at least 48 hours, and complex matters may need longer.

4. The hearing

An impartial chairperson runs the hearing. A typical order is:

  • the chairperson opens the hearing and confirms the employee’s rights;
  • the charges are read and the employee pleads guilty or not guilty;
  • the employer’s representative (the initiator) presents evidence and witnesses, who may be cross-examined;
  • the employee presents their side, evidence and witnesses;
  • both sides make closing arguments.

5. Finding and sanction

The chairperson decides whether the employee is guilty on each charge. If so, both sides may address mitigating and aggravating factors, such as length of service, disciplinary record and the seriousness of the misconduct. The sanction may range from a written warning to a final warning or dismissal. Dismissal is generally reserved for serious misconduct or repeated offences.

The outcome and the reasons should be given to the employee in writing.

6. Appeal and the CCMA

If the disciplinary code provides for an internal appeal, the employee may appeal. An employee who believes the dismissal was unfair can also refer a dispute to the CCMA or bargaining council, generally within 30 days.

Tips for employers

  • Follow your own disciplinary code. Inconsistency is a common reason dismissals are found unfair.
  • Use a chairperson with no involvement in the matter.
  • Keep a proper record of the hearing.
