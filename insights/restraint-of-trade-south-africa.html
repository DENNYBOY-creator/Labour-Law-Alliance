Are Restraint of Trade Clauses Enforceable in South Africa?

Restraints of trade are enforceable in South Africa, but only if they are reasonable. Here is how the courts decide and what to check before you sign.

Compliance & HR · 27 November 2026 · 2 min read · By Labour Law Alliance

A restraint of trade clause stops an employee from competing with their former employer, or working for a competitor, for a period after they leave. These clauses are common in South African employment contracts, and they are often misunderstood by both employers and employees.

The starting point

Since the Appellate Division’s decision in Magna Alloys and Research (SA) (Pty) Ltd v Ellis (1984), restraints of trade are valid and enforceable in principle, unless they are unreasonable or against the public interest. A party who wants to escape a restraint generally carries the burden of showing that it is unreasonable.

How the courts decide reasonableness

Courts usually ask questions along these lines (often referred to from Basson v Chilwan, 1993):

  • Does the employer have an interest that deserves protection?
  • Is that interest being threatened by the former employee?
  • Does the employer’s interest outweigh the employee’s interest in being economically active and earning a living?
  • Is there any other public interest that affects the balance?

Protectable interests

The interests courts will usually protect are:

  • Confidential information and trade secrets, such as pricing, formulas or strategy; and
  • Customer connections, where the employee built relationships that could be used to take customers away.

A restraint that simply aims to prevent competition, or stops someone using general skills and experience, is unlikely to be enforced.

Duration, area and scope

Even where there is a protectable interest, the restraint must be reasonable in how long it lasts, the geographic area it covers and the activities it prohibits. A two-year, nationwide ban on a junior employee working anywhere in an industry will be harder to justify than a shorter, narrower restraint on a senior employee with key client relationships. Courts may enforce a restraint only partly, for example for a shorter period.

Tips for employers

  • Tailor restraints to the role. One standard clause for every employee is a red flag.
  • Identify the specific interest you are protecting.
  • Keep duration and area to what is genuinely needed.

Tips for employees

  • Read the restraint before you sign, and ask for changes if it is too broad.
  • Before taking a new job, get advice on whether your restraint is likely to be enforced.
